\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

How many voters are needed before their pairwise majorities can produce
every tournament? For odd \(k\), let \(N(k)\) be the least number of
vertices on which some tournament arises from no \(k\) voters. Only
\(N(3) = 8\) is known (Shepardson and Tovey 2009; Eggermont et
al.~2013). For five voters the best bounds were \(11 \le N(5)\)
(Bachmeier et al.~2019) and \(N(5) \le 41\) (Eggermont et al.~2013,
p.~271; Bachmeier et al.~2019). We show \(13 \le N(5) \le 22\). The
upper bound comes from nine tournaments on 22 vertices that five voters
cannot produce: eight one-vertex deletions of doubly regular tournaments
on 23 vertices, including every deletion of one of them, and one arc
reversal. The Paley tournament on 23 vertices, which Bachmeier et
al.~(2019) left open after six weeks of SAT solving, is beyond five
voters, and so is every other doubly regular tournament on 23 vertices.
The lower bound comes from an analysis of all tournaments on 12
vertices. An argument that searches no profiles shows that the Paley
tournament on 43 vertices is also beyond five voters. Its
predictability, the largest share of the voters that a profile can give
to every majority at once, exceeds the threshold \(3/5\) by only
\(2/1505\), so this result refutes the conjecture that a predictability
above the threshold lets five voters produce a tournament. Our method is
a search that places one vertex at a time and exploits the tournament's
symmetries. We certify four of the negative results with
machine-checkable proofs, and a public repository reproduces every
result.

\textbf{Keywords:} majority voting; tournaments; McGarvey's theorem;
Paley tournaments; constraint search; SAT

\subsection{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

Let \(T\) be a \emph{tournament} on a vertex set \(V\) with \(|V| = n\):
a digraph in which every unordered pair \(\{i, j\}\) carries exactly one
of the arcs \(i \to j\) and \(j \to i\). A \emph{profile} of \(k\)
\emph{voters} is a list of \(k\) linear orders \(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_k\)
of \(V\), and its \emph{majority tournament} has the arc \(i \to j\)
whenever \(|\{ v : i \prec_{\pi_v} j \}| > k/2\); for odd \(k\) this
decides every pair. We say that \(T\) is \emph{\(k\)-inducible} if it is
the majority tournament of some profile of \(k\) voters, which we then
call a \emph{witness} for \(T\). McGarvey (1953) proved that every
tournament is \(k\)-inducible for some \(k\), and counting profiles
shows that for fixed \(k\) almost all large tournaments are not. For odd
\(k\) we write \(N(k)\) for the least order of a tournament that is not
\(k\)-inducible.

Only \(N(3) = 8\) is known (Shepardson and Tovey 2009; Eggermont,
Hurkens and Woeginger 2013). For \(k = 5\) the bounds were
\(11 \le N(5) \le 41\) (Bachmeier et al.~2019; Eggermont et al.~2013,
p.~271), the upper one by counting, and the smallest explicit tournament
known not to be \(5\)-inducible had over \(6 \times 10^{8}\) vertices
(Fidler 2011; Bachmeier et al.~2019). We show that
\(13 \le N(5) \le 22\); Section 1.3 lists our results, and Figure 1
shows one of the eight tournaments on \(22\) vertices that give the
upper bound.

What these \emph{obstructions} (tournaments that are not
\(5\)-inducible) share is balance rather than symmetry: none of the
\(37\) doubly regular tournaments on \(23\) vertices, in which every
vertex has out-degree \(11\) and every two vertices the same number of
common out-neighbours, is \(5\)-inducible, although \(32\) of them have
a trivial automorphism group (Section 6.4).

The \emph{support} \(c(e)\) of an arc \(e: i \to j\) is the number of
voters ranking \(i\) above \(j\), and its \emph{margin} is
\(2c(e) - k\), so for \(k = 5\) every margin in a witness is \(1\),
\(3\) or \(5\). A witness has \emph{margin at most \(M\)} if every arc
does, and \emph{unit margin} if every margin is \(1\). Our companion
paper (Chindelevitch and Harutyunyan 2026), whose definitions we use,
conjectures that this margin hierarchy is strict; for \(k = 5\),
\(P_{19}\) is \(5\)-inducible with margin at most \(3\) but not with
unit margin (Section 6.2). Reversing every voter reverses every arc and
changes no support, so \(T\) and its converse \(\bar{T}\) are
\(k\)-inducible for the same \(k\) and at the same margins; we count a
family enumerated in full up to isomorphism, and a family that our
searches generated up to isomorphism and converse.

The \emph{predictability} \(\alpha^{*}(T)\) (Shepardson and Tovey 2009)
is the largest \(\alpha > 1/2\) such that some profile, on any number
\(t\) of voters, supports every arc of \(T\) with at least \(\alpha t\)
of them. A \(k\)-voter witness supports every arc with more than \(k/2\)
voters, so \(k\)-inducibility forces \(\alpha^{*}(T) \ge (k+1)/2k\),
which is \(3/5\) at \(k = 5\); the converse, implicit in their work, we
call the \emph{threshold conjecture}. Our companion paper refutes it at
\(k = 3\), where it fails only at the threshold; at \(k = 5\) it fails
strictly above it, for \(P_{23}\) with \(\alpha^{*} = 7/11\) (Section
6.1) and for \(P_{43}\), where \(\alpha^{*}\) exceeds \(3/5\) by only
\(2/1505\) (Section 4).

\subsubsection{1.1 Special properties of Paley
tournaments}\label{special-properties-of-paley-tournaments}

The Paley tournament \(P_q\), for a prime \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), has
vertex set \(\mathbb{F}_q\) and an arc from \(i\) to \(j\) whenever
\(j - i\) is a nonzero square, or \emph{quadratic residue}; we write
\(\mathrm{QR}\) for the set of these, so that \(i \to j\) exactly when
\(j - i \in
\mathrm{QR}\). As everywhere in this paper, vertices are numbered from
\(1\), vertex \(i + 1\) being the field element \(i\). It is
\emph{doubly regular}: every vertex has out-degree \((q-1)/2\) and every
ordered pair of vertices has exactly \((q-3)/4\) common out-neighbours.
Its automorphism group \(\mathrm{Aut}(P_q)\) (Section 2) has order
\(q(q-1)/2\). This makes \(P_q\) \emph{arc-transitive}, meaning that for
every pair of arcs \(e, e'\) of \(P_q\) there is an automorphism of
\(P_q\) that takes \(e\) into \(e'\). It is also \emph{self-converse},
meaning that it is isomorphic to its own converse; the relevant mapping
is multiplication by \(-1\), which is easily checked to not be an
automorphism when \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\).

\subsubsection{1.2 Neither SAT nor the slack argument settles
Paley(23)}\label{neither-sat-nor-the-slack-argument-settles-paley23}

Paley tournaments are natural candidates for non-inducibility because
they are far from being transitive, and because their symmetry makes an
otherwise hopeless search merely very hard. Bachmeier et al.~(2019) call
them \emph{quadratic residue tournaments}, with the converse
orientation, which changes no result here. Their SAT study shows that
\(P_3\) and \(P_7\) are \(3\)-inducible and that \(P_{11}\) and
\(P_{19}\) are \(5\)-inducible but not \(3\)-inducible, and then stops
at \(P_{23}\), on which their solver did not terminate within six weeks.

Nor does the argument by which we settle \(P_{43}\) (Section 4), which
searches no profiles. It rests on the \emph{slack}, the amount by which
five voters can collectively fall short of the most arcs one order can
agree with; the slack is proportional to \(\alpha^{*} - 3/5\), and it is
\(6\) at \(q = 43\) but \(46\) at \(q = 23\) (Table 2), which leaves the
voters of a candidate profile for \(P_{23}\) far too much room.

\subsubsection{1.3 Contributions}\label{contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{There are non-\(5\)-inducible tournaments on \(22\) vertices,
  so \(N(5) \le 22\)} (Section 6.5). We write \(\Delta_i\) for the
  \(i\)-th of the \(37\) doubly regular tournaments on \(23\) vertices
  in the catalogue of McKay (2026), and \(\Delta_i - v_j\) for
  \(\Delta_i\) with its vertex \(v_j\) deleted. Eight such tournaments,
  pairwise non-isomorphic even up to converse, are not \(5\)-inducible
  and are vertex-critical; three are the one-vertex deletions of
  \(\Delta_1\), and reversing an arc of one of them,
  \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\), gives a ninth obstruction.
\item
  \textbf{\(P_{23}\) is not \(5\)-inducible} (Section 6.1). The SAT
  solver of Bachmeier et al.~(2019) did not terminate on it within six
  weeks; our search settles it in twenty-two hours on a laptop. We also
  decide \(P_{31}\).
\item
  \textbf{Counting gives \(N(5) \le 38\), and \(P_{43}\) is not
  \(5\)-inducible} (Sections 3 and 4). The counting bounds of Bachmeier
  et al.~(2019) improve for every odd \(k \le 21\), and \(P_{43}\),
  whose predictability exceeds \(3/5\) by only \(2/1505\), refutes the
  threshold conjecture at \(k = 5\) strictly above the threshold.
\item
  \textbf{\(P_{19}\) is \(5\)-inducible, but not with unit margin}
  (Section 6.2). At \(k = 3\) such tournaments first appear at order
  \(9\), after \(N(3) = 8\) (see our companion paper); at \(k = 5\) they
  appear by order \(19\), before \(N(5)\) unless \(N(5) \le 19\), which
  the search of Appendix C makes unlikely.
\item
  \textbf{\(P_{19}\) and \(P_{23}\) are arc-critical, and \(P_{31}\) and
  \(P_{43}\) are not vertex-critical} (Sections 6.1 and 6.3). Reversing
  an arc of \(P_{19}\) restores \(5\)-inducibility at unit margin and
  reversing an arc of \(P_{23}\) restores it unrestricted, while
  \(P_{31}\) and \(P_{43}\) minus a vertex are still not
  \(5\)-inducible.
\item
  \textbf{Four negative results are formally certified} (Appendix B):
  \(P_{19}\) at unit margin, and \(P_{23}\), \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\) and
  \(\Delta_6 - v_{5}\). Each subproblem (\emph{cube}) is refuted by the
  SAT solver CaDiCaL with a machine-checkable \emph{LRAT} proof (Section
  1.4), rechecked by a separate program.
\item
  \textbf{Every tournament on \(12\) vertices is \(5\)-inducible, even
  at unit margin} (Appendix A), by settling all \(2{,}048\) one-vertex
  extensions of each of the \(\approx 9.0 \times 10^{8}\) tournaments on
  \(11\) vertices.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{1.4 Related work}\label{related-work}

This paper builds on two others. Our companion paper (Chindelevitch and
Harutyunyan 2026) settles the three-voter questions from which this one
starts, and we use its definitions. Bachmeier et al.~(2019) is the prior
art for both the \emph{bounds} we improve and the \emph{method}: besides
the bounds above, it brought SAT to bear on \(k\)-majority digraphs,
characterized the \(3\)-majority case building on Dushnik and Miller
(1941), and established hardness results for several voting rules with a
constant number of voters. Without a bound on \(k\), every tournament on
\(n\) vertices is \(k\)-inducible with \(k = O(n/\log n)\) (Erdős and
Moser 1964).

The value of \(N(3)\) is settled. Shepardson and Tovey (2009) introduced
the predictability \(\alpha^{*}(T)\) defined above. A \(3\)-voter
profile is in particular a \(2/3\)-supermajority representation, so
\(\alpha^{*}(T) < 2/3\) rules out \(3\)-inducibility; their \(8\)-vertex
tournament has \(\alpha^{*} = 13/20\), which gives \(N(3) \le 8\). They
also prove that every tournament on at most \(7\) vertices has
\(\alpha^{*} \in \{2/3, 1\}\), and Eggermont, Hurkens and Woeginger
(2013) then show by direct computation using an integer linear
programming (ILP) formulation that every tournament on \(7\) vertices is
\(3\)-inducible, and every tournament on \(8\) or \(9\) vertices is
\(5\)-inducible, yielding \(N(3) = 8\) and \(N(5) \ge 10\).

So at \(k = 3\) predictability decides exactly at \(N(3)\); at \(k = 5\)
it does not, as \(P_{23}\), \(P_{31}\) and \(P_{43}\) show (Table 2).

Our certified computation (Appendix B) is standard. It splits the
problem into independent subproblems, using the \emph{cube-and-conquer}
method (Heule et al.~2012): a SAT instance is split by fixing a few
variables at a time, giving many partial assignments called
\emph{cubes}, and each cube is handed to the solver as a problem of its
own. We adopt the standard of evidence set by the Boolean Pythagorean
triples proof (Heule et al.~2016), later re-checked with a formally
verified checker (Cruz-Filipe et al.~2019), in which every subproblem
emits a machine-checkable refutation that an independent checker
validates. Our refutations are in the LRAT format (Cruz-Filipe et
al.~2017) (Appendix B.1), a format in which the solver records each
clause it derives so that an independent checker can replay and confirm
the entire derivation.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 sets out the
notation. Section 3 bounds \(N(k)\) by counting, and Section 4 proves
that \(P_{43}\) is not \(5\)-inducible by an argument that searches no
profiles. Section 5 describes the search: its symmetry reductions, the
algorithm and what makes it fast. Section 6 presents the results of the
search: the Paley tournaments (Sections 6.1 to 6.3), the other
structured families (Section 6.4), and the eight \(22\)-vertex
obstructions that give \(N(5) \le 22\) (Section 6.5). Section 7 states
what remains open and describes the reproducibility material.

\subsection{2. Preliminaries}\label{preliminaries}

In this section we fix the notation used throughout.

Throughout, \(T\) is a tournament on \(V = \{1, \dots, n\}\) and
\(k = 5\) unless stated otherwise. We write \(N^{+}(v)\) and
\(N^{-}(v)\) for the out- and in-neighbourhoods of \(v\). A tournament
is \emph{regular} if \(|N^{+}(v)| = |N^{-}(v)|\) for each \(v \in V\);
this can only happen for \(n\) odd. For even \(n\) it is
\emph{near-regular} if every out-degree is \(n/2 - 1\) or \(n/2\); since
the out-degrees sum to \(\binom{n}{2}\), exactly \(n/2\) vertices then
have each of the two. Following our companion paper, \(D_n\), \(R_n\)
and \(S_n\) denote the numbers of isomorphism classes of, respectively,
all tournaments, regular tournaments and self-converse tournaments on
\(n\) vertices, which are OEIS A000568, A096368 and A002785 (OEIS
Foundation Inc.~2026a, 2026c, 2026b). For any \(S \subseteq V\), the
sub-tournament of \(T\) induced by \(S\), written \(T[S]\), is the
tournament obtained by restricting \(T\) to the vertices in \(S\) and
the arcs between those vertices.

We call a tournament \(T\) \emph{vertex-critical} for \(k\)-inducibility
at margin \(M\) if \(T\) is not \(k\)-inducible at margin \(M\), but
\(T - v\) is \(k\)-inducible at margin \(M\) for every vertex \(v\).
Similarly, we call \(T\) \emph{arc-critical} for such a property if
\(T\) has it, but any \(T'\) obtained from \(T\) by reversing an arc
does not.

An \emph{automorphism} of a tournament \(T\) is a permutation of its
vertices carrying every arc to an arc; these form a group
\(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\) under composition. The identity is always an
automorphism; any other automorphism is \emph{non-trivial}, and \(T\) is
\emph{rigid} when it has none. For a permutation group \(\Gamma\) on a
set \(X\), the \emph{orbit} of \(x\) is
\(\Gamma x = \{\gamma(x) : \gamma \in \Gamma\}\), and the orbits
partition \(X\). The group is \emph{transitive} on \(X\) when there is a
single orbit, that is, when for any \(x, x' \in X\) there is a
\(g \in \Gamma\) with \(g(x) = x'\). We use orbits of
\(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\) on the vertices, on the arcs, and on the
\emph{ordered pairs} of distinct vertices, where \(\gamma\) sends
\((u,v)\) to \((\gamma(u), \gamma(v))\). An ordered pair is either an
arc or the reverse of one, so that third action has at least two orbits.

The next section uses this notation to bound \(N(k)\) by counting.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{3. Counting bounds on
\(N(k)\)}{3. Counting bounds on N(k)}}\label{counting-bounds-on-nk}

In this section we bound \(N(k)\) by counting, without exhibiting a
tournament: some tournament on \(38\) vertices is not \(5\)-inducible,
and the counting bounds of Bachmeier et al.~(2019) improve for every odd
\(k \le 21\).

Comparing counts of profiles and tournaments gives \(N(5) \le 41\)
(Eggermont et al.~2013, p.~271; Bachmeier et al.~2019). Restricting the
count to \emph{regular} tournaments tightens this to \(N(5) \le 39\),
and to \emph{near-regular} tournaments to \(N(5) \le 38\), because four
voters determine a tournament of prescribed out-degrees.

\subsubsection{3.1 Four voters determine a tournament with given
out-degrees}\label{four-voters-determine-a-tournament-with-given-out-degrees}

The fifth voter of a profile is constrained only on the pairs that the
other four split evenly, and there a prescribed out-degree sequence
leaves it no choice.

\textbf{Proposition 3.1 (completion uniqueness).} \emph{Fix odd \(k\),
linear orders \(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_{k-1}\) on \([n]\) and a sequence
\(d \colon [n] \to \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\). At most one tournament with
out-degree sequence \(d\) is the majority tournament of
\(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_{k-1}\) and some \(k\)-th order, and it can be found
in \(O(n^2)\) time.}

\emph{Proof.} Pairs that \(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_{k-1}\) split with margin
at least \(2\) are decided whatever \(\pi_k\) does; the evenly split
pairs form a graph \(D\) on which the majority follows \(\pi_k\). The
tournament is therefore determined by the orientation \(R\) of \(D\)
that \(\pi_k\) induces, which is acyclic, and every acyclic orientation
of \(D\) arises from some \(\pi_k\). The sequence \(d\) fixes the
out-degree \(b(x)\) of each \(x\) in \(R\): \(d(x)\) minus the number of
decided arcs leaving \(x\). An acyclic orientation has a sink, a vertex
with \(b(x) = 0\), and such a vertex must have all its remaining edges
oriented into it. Repeatedly deleting a vertex with \(b(x) = 0\),
orienting its remaining edges into it and decrementing its neighbours'
budgets therefore either fails, when no such vertex exists or a budget
turns negative, and then no completion exists, or orients every edge in
the only possible way; the reverse deletion order is then a valid
\(\pi_k\) (Kahn 1962). Each vertex and edge is handled once, in
\(O(n^2)\) time. \(\blacksquare\)

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{3.2 Counting near-regular tournaments
gives
\(N(5) \le 38\)}{3.2 Counting near-regular tournaments gives N(5) \textbackslash le 38}}\label{counting-near-regular-tournaments-gives-n5-le-38}

For even \(n\) there are no regular tournaments, but the near-regular
ones (Section 2) substitute for them: deleting a vertex \(v\) from a
regular tournament on \(n + 1\) vertices leaves a near-regular
tournament on \(n\), and each near-regular tournament arises from
exactly one regular tournament in this way, by re-adding \(v\) beating
the \(n/2\) vertices of out-degree \(n/2 - 1\). Hence
\[\#\{\text{near-regular tournaments on } n\} \;=\; R^{L}_{n+1}, \tag{3.1}\]
where \(R^{L}_{\nu}\) is the number of \emph{labelled} regular
tournaments on \(\nu\) vertices, the counting taking place in the
labelled world, unlike the isomorphism count \(R_n\).

\textbf{Theorem 3.2.} \emph{Some near-regular tournament on 38 vertices
is not 5-inducible; hence \(N(5) \le 38\).}

\emph{Proof.} Deleting a voter from a witness shows, by Proposition 3.1,
that a \(5\)-inducible tournament with a prescribed out-degree sequence
is determined by a \(4\)-multiset of orders. The near-regular sequences
on even \(n\) number \(\binom{n}{n/2}\), so
\[\log_2 \#\{\text{5-inducible near-regular on } n\} \;\le\;
\log_2\Bigl[\binom{n}{n/2}\binom{n! + 3}{4}\Bigr] \;<\; 624.7 \quad (n = 38).\]
By (3.1) and Schrijver's (1983) lower bound on Eulerian orientations of
\(K_{39}\), of which the regular tournaments on \(39\) vertices are an
instance,
\[\log_2 \#\{\text{near-regular on } 38\} \;=\; \log_2 R^{L}_{39} \;\ge\;
\log_2\Bigl[2^{-\binom{39}{2}} \binom{38}{19}^{39}\Bigr] \;>\; 625.5 ,\]
which exceeds \(624.7\). \(\blacksquare\)

The regular count alone gives only \(N(5) \le 39\):
\(\log_2 R^{L}_{39} > 625.5 > 610.8 >
\log_2 \binom{39! + 3}{4}\), so all but a \(2^{-14.8}\) fraction of
regular tournaments on \(39\) vertices are not \(5\)-inducible. At
\(k = 3\) the same argument shows only that some regular tournaments on
\(17\) or more vertices are not \(3\)-inducible, far weaker than
\(N(3) = 8\).

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{3.3 The counting bound improves for every
odd
\(k \le 21\)}{3.3 The counting bound improves for every odd k \textbackslash le 21}}\label{the-counting-bound-improves-for-every-odd-k-le-21}

The argument is not specific to five voters: Proposition 3.1 holds for
every odd \(k\), and the near-regular bijection (3.1) does not depend on
\(k\). Counting both classes gives:

\textbf{Theorem 3.3 (all odd \(k\)).} \emph{For odd \(k \ge 3\): (a) at
most \(\binom{n! + k - 2}{k - 1}\) regular tournaments on odd \([n]\)
are \(k\)-inducible, so a non-\(k\)-inducible regular tournament exists
once \(R^{L}_{n} > \binom{n! + k - 2}{k - 1}\); (b) at most
\(\binom{n}{n/2}\binom{n! + k - 2}{k - 1}\) near-regular tournaments on
even \([n]\) are \(k\)-inducible, so a non-\(k\)-inducible near-regular
tournament exists once
\(R^{L}_{n+1} > \binom{n}{n/2}\binom{n! + k - 2}{k - 1}\), using in both
cases Schrijver's bound
\(R^{L}_{\nu} \ge 2^{-\binom{\nu}{2}}\binom{\nu-1}{(\nu-1)/2}^{\nu}\)
(odd \(\nu\)).}

\subsection{4. Paley(43) is not
5-inducible}\label{paley43-is-not-5-inducible}

\(P_{43}\) is not \(5\)-inducible, although its predictability exceeds
the threshold \(3/5\). Section 3 showed that a non-5-inducible
tournament exists on \(38\) vertices; in this section we prove that
\(P_{43}\) is one, by an argument that searches no profiles. Among the
Paley tournaments of Table 2, \(P_{43}\) is the only one that the margin
of its predictability over \(3/5\) settles on its own: that margin is
just \(2/1505\), and it leaves five voters too little room. \(P_{23}\),
with \(\alpha^{*} = 7/11\), refutes the threshold conjecture further
above the threshold (Section 6.1), but only through the search. We write
\(P = P_{43}\) throughout the section.

\textbf{Theorem 4.1.} \emph{\(P = P_{43}\) satisfies
\(\alpha^{*}(P) = \mathrm{MAS}/C = 543/903
= 181/301 > 3/5\), yet \(P\) is not 5-inducible. Hence
\(\alpha^{*} > \frac{k+1}{2k}\) does not imply \(k\)-inducibility, and
\(N(5) \le 43\) with an explicit tournament.}

Here \(\mathrm{MAS}(T)\) is the maximum number of arcs of \(T\) that a
single linear order agrees with. An arc \(u \to v\) is \emph{forward} in
an order \(\pi\) if \(\pi\) ranks \(u\) above \(v\) and \emph{backward}
otherwise, and \(\mathrm{fwd}_T(\pi)\) is the set of forward arcs. The
identity \(\alpha^{*} = \mathrm{MAS}/C\) holds because \(P\) is
arc-transitive, that is, \(\mathrm{Aut}(P)\) acts transitively on its
arcs. No order has more than \(\mathrm{MAS}\) forward arcs, so in any
profile the average support of an arc is at most a fraction
\(\mathrm{MAS}/C\) of the voters, and the smallest support is no larger.
Conversely, the profile formed by the images under \(\mathrm{Aut}(P)\)
of one order with \(\mathrm{MAS}\) forward arcs gives every arc exactly
that fraction (the symmetrization lemma of our companion paper).
Throughout, \(q = 43\), \(C = \binom{q}{2} = 903\), the number of cyclic
triangles is \(N_{\Delta} = (q^3 - q)/24 = 3311\), and
\(|\mathrm{Aut}(P)| = q(q-1)/2 = 903\).

\subsubsection{4.1 The slack forces two near-optimal
voters}\label{the-slack-forces-two-near-optimal-voters}

In a witness every arc is forward for at least three of the five voters,
so the voters have at least \(3C\) forward arcs between them, while none
has more than \(\mathrm{MAS}\). The \emph{slack}
\[\mathrm{slack}_5(T) \;=\; 5\,\mathrm{MAS}(T) - 3C\] therefore bounds
how far the voters can collectively fall short of the maximum. For an
arc-transitive \(T\) it equals \(5C\,(\alpha^{*}(T) - 3/5)\), so it
grows in proportion to \(C\) whenever the predictability stands off from
the threshold. A subset dynamic program modulo \(\mathrm{Aut}(P)\) with
a meet-in-the-middle step (Appendix D) gives \(\mathrm{MAS}(P) = 543\),
so \(\mathrm{slack}_5(P) = 2715 - 2709 = 6\), the smallest value in
Table 2; the others are at least \(7\).

For an inducing profile \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_5)\), let
\(f_i = |\mathrm{fwd}_P(\pi_i)|\) and relabel the voters so that
\(f_1 \ge f_2 \ge \cdots \ge f_5\). Then
\(\sum_{i=1}^{5} f_i \ge 3C = 2709\) and \(f_1 \le 543\), so the other
four sum to at least \(2166\), and \(4 f_2 \ge 2166\),
i.e.~\(f_2 \ge 542\). The \emph{level} of an order \(\pi\) is
\(\delta(\pi) = \mathrm{MAS} - |\mathrm{fwd}_P(\pi)|\), so \textbf{at
least two voters lie at level \(\le 1\)}, and the five levels sum to at
most \(\mathrm{slack}_5(P) = 6\).

A cyclic triangle is \emph{double-backed} by an order that ranks two of
its three arcs backward, and the \emph{double-back set}
\(\mathrm{DB}(\pi)\) of \(\pi\) is the set of cyclic triangles it
double-backs.

\textbf{Lemma 4.2 (co-backing).} \emph{In a \(5\)-voter witness, no
cyclic triangle is double-backed by two voters.}

\emph{Proof.} Each arc of the triangle is ranked backward by \(\le 2\)
of the 5 voters, so there are at most six backward incidences; each
voter ranks \(\ge 1\) arc of a cyclic triangle backward (a linear order
cannot agree with all three arcs of a cycle); if \(d\) voters
double-back, incidences \(= 5 + d \le 6\), so \(d \le 1\).
\(\blacksquare\)

The five voters' double-back sets are therefore pairwise disjoint, so
the two voters at level \(\le 1\) have disjoint double-back sets.
\textbf{If no such pair exists, \(P\) is not 5-inducible}, and this is a
finite criterion.

\subsubsection{4.2 No two near-optimal orders have disjoint double-back
sets}\label{no-two-near-optimal-orders-have-disjoint-double-back-sets}

The orders of level at most \(1\), which we call the \emph{shell},
number \(1{,}662{,}696{,}609\), in \(1{,}841{,}303\) orbits of
\(\mathrm{Aut}(P)\) (Appendix E). We decide the criterion by checking
every pair, which two reductions make practical:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A window of 24 vertices.} We fix the window
  \(\mathcal{W} = \{0, 1, \dots, 23\}\) and its 538 cyclic triangles.
  The restriction of \(\mathrm{DB}(\pi)\) to these triangles depends
  only on how \(\pi\) ranks \(\mathcal{W}\), so the shell has only
  \(4{,}709{,}640\) distinct restrictions, and two orders with disjoint
  double-back sets have disjoint restrictions; only the
  \(5{,}092{,}111\) disjoint pairs of restrictions need the exact check.
\item
  \textbf{Orbit representatives on one side.}
  \(\mathrm{DB}(\sigma \pi) = \sigma(\mathrm{DB}(\pi))\) for
  \(\sigma \in \mathrm{Aut}(P)\), where \(\sigma \pi\) ranks
  \(\sigma(v)\) where \(\pi\) ranks \(v\), because \(\sigma\) maps
  forward arcs of \(\pi\) to forward arcs of \(\sigma \pi\). Overlap
  sizes are thus \(\mathrm{Aut}\)-invariant, so testing one
  representative per orbit against the shell suffices, saving a factor
  of \(903\).
\end{itemize}

The exact check compared all \(4{,}376{,}325{,}129\) pairs of an orbit
representative and a shell order whose restrictions are disjoint, and
\textbf{none has disjoint double-back sets}; the smallest overlap is
\(68\) triangles. With the co-backing lemma, this proves Theorem 4.1.
\(\blacksquare\)

\subsection{5. The search}\label{the-search}

In this section we describe the search that decides the instances of
Section 6: the symmetry reductions it relies on (Section 5.1), the
algorithm (Section 5.2) and what makes it fast (Section 5.3). Appendix F
proves the reductions and explains why integer programming and SAT alone
fall short here.

\subsubsection{5.1 Base states and symmetry
reductions}\label{base-states-and-symmetry-reductions}

Every search starts from a small set \(B\) of vertices, the \emph{base},
and a \emph{base state} of \(B\): \(k\) linear orders of \(B\) whose
majorities reproduce \(T[B]\) at the margin being asked for, counted up
to relabelling the voters. Each base state is a self-contained
subproblem, namely whether the remaining \(n - |B|\) vertices can be
inserted so that every arc of \(T\) gets the right support, and \(T\) is
\(k\)-inducible exactly when some base state extends to a witness. The
number of base states depends on \(|B|\), on \(T[B]\) and on the margin,
but not on \(n\): the five-vertex bases we use on the Paley tournaments
have \(8{,}031\) of them in both \(P_{23}\) and \(P_{47}\).

Two symmetries act on witnesses: the \(k!\) relabellings of the voters,
which form the symmetric group \(S_k\), and \(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\) acting
on the vertices. A permutation \(\sigma\) of the voters acts on a
profile \(\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_k)\) by reordering it, and a
permutation \(h\) of \(V\) acts on a linear order by relabelling, so
that \(h\pi_j\) ranks \(h(u)\) above \(h(w)\) exactly when \(\pi_j\)
ranks \(u\) above \(w\), and on a profile voter by voter. The two
actions commute, so the group \(S_k \times \mathrm{Aut}(T)\) acts on
profiles. It maps witnesses to witnesses: permuting voters does not
change any margin, and the margin of \(h(u) \to h(w)\) after relabelling
by an automorphism \(h\) equals that of \(u \to w\) before. A witness
\emph{extends} the base state formed by its orders restricted to \(B\),
and the \emph{restriction} of a base state of \(B\) to \(C \subseteq B\)
is the multiset of its orders restricted to \(C\). Every reduction we
use is a case of the following lemma, which Appendix F.2 proves.

\textbf{Lemma 5.1 (symmetry reductions).} \emph{Let \(T\) be
\(k\)-inducible.}

\emph{(a) (Orbit principle.) Let \(G\) be a subgroup of
\(S_k \times \mathrm{Aut}(T)\) acting on a set \(\Omega\), let
\(\varphi\) map profiles to \(\Omega\) with
\(\varphi(g\pi) = g\,\varphi(\pi)\) for every \(g \in G\), and let
\(R \subseteq \Omega\) meet every \(G\)-orbit. Then \(T\) has a witness
\(\pi\) with \(\varphi(\pi) \in R\).}

\emph{(b) (Orbit anchoring.) If \((a_1, b_1), \dots, (a_m, b_m)\)
represent the orbits of \(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\) on ordered pairs of distinct
vertices, then \(T\) has a witness in which, for some \(i\), some voter
ranks \(a_i\) first and \(b_i\) second.}

\emph{(c) (Vertex anchoring.) If \(v_1, \dots, v_r\) represent the
vertex orbits, a voter of some witness ranks some \(v_i\) first.}

\emph{(d) (Voter sorting.) For any total order on linear orders, \(T\)
has a witness whose \(k\) orders are non-decreasing, and this witness
can also be taken to satisfy (b) or (c).}

\emph{(e) (Base-stabilizer quotient.) Let \(H\) be a subgroup of
\(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\) mapping \(C \subseteq B\) onto itself, choose one
base state of \(C\) from each \(H\)-orbit, and call a base state of
\(B\) chosen if its restriction to \(C\) is chosen. Then some chosen
base state of \(B\) extends to a witness. If every element of \(H\)
fixes each representative of (b), or of (c), then that witness can also
be taken to satisfy (b), or (c), respectively.}

We call a witness satisfying (b) or (c) \emph{anchored}. Sorting the
voters (part (d)) is built into the definition of a base state and
divides their number by almost \(120\). Anchoring discards base states
outright when every representative lies in \(B\), leaving \(2{,}591\) of
the \(8{,}031\) \emph{live} on a Paley tournament, and otherwise it
prunes during the search. The quotient (e) divides the work by up to
\(|H|\). The two combine when \(H\) fixes every representative, so we
take \(H\) to be their common stabilizer, and together they use all of
\(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\) (Table F.1).

\subsubsection{5.2 The placement search}\label{the-placement-search}

The search maintains a partial profile: a set \(S \subseteq V\) of
\emph{placed} vertices together with, for each voter, a linear order of
\(S\). It begins from a base state, which places \(B\), and inserts one
unplaced vertex at a time. Inserting \(v\) means choosing, for each
voter independently, a slot among the \(|S| + 1\) available; the
\emph{domain} \(D(v \mid S)\) is the set of \(k\)-tuples of slots
consistent with every arc between \(v\) and \(S\). A partial profile is
extendable only if every unplaced vertex has a non-empty domain, and
domains shrink as \(S\) grows. Algorithm 1 gives the search, and Figure
F.2 (Appendix F.3) runs it on \(P_7\) at \(k = 3\). The base states are
searched in parallel; the verdict is positive as soon as one yields a
witness, and negative only when every live base state has been searched
to exhaustion. The certified refutations of Appendix B use the same
decomposition, handing each live base state to a conflict-driven
clause-learning (CDCL) solver as a \emph{cube} (the partial assignment
fixing the voters' rankings of \(B\)), so the two share their symmetry
reductions and differ only in what refutes a leaf.

\subsubsection{5.3 What makes the search
fast}\label{what-makes-the-search-fast}

Five choices make the search fast; the second is the one that made
\(n = 19\) and \(n = 23\) reasonable to compute.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A dynamic variable order.} At every node we insert the
  unplaced vertex of smallest domain (the minimum-remaining-values, or
  MRV, rule), breaking ties toward the vertex whose arcs to \(S\) are
  most imbalanced. On Paley instances a static order is not competitive,
  because how constrained a vertex is depends strongly on which part of
  the tournament has already been placed.
\item
  \textbf{Incremental domain refinement.} A child's domains are derived
  from its parent's by \texttt{REFINE}, in time proportional to the
  surviving tuples rather than to the whole slot space, and they live in
  a stack-disciplined arena indexed by (depth, vertex), so that
  backtracking is a pointer reset. Like for like this is worth a factor
  of about \(110\) in wall time.
\item
  \textbf{Symmetry reductions.} Base states are sorted and taken one per
  orbit of \(H\), and the anchors are tested at every node once all
  representatives are placed (Section 5.1).
\item
  \textbf{Early termination and caching.} A per-voter agreement bound,
  counting the agreement between a voter and the arcs between \(v\) and
  \(S\) two ways, proves \(D(v \mid S)\) empty in \(O(k|S|)\) time
  without enumerating it. Base-state counts depend only on the arc
  pattern of \(T[B]\), so they are reused across every copy of the same
  base class and across every \(q\).
\item
  \textbf{Choosing the base.} The fewest live base states is not the
  fastest choice: at \(q = 31\) the class with \(4{,}007\) live states
  beat the one with \(2{,}537\), whose states are individually slower,
  so we rank the twelve five-vertex classes by the live count times a
  sampled time per state.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{6. Results}\label{results}

In this section we present our results, first for the Paley tournaments
and the other structured families, then for the eight obstructions on 22
vertices. Table B.3 in Appendix B.2 lists what each result rests on.

The two regimes we explore are \emph{unrestricted} (or plain majority)
inducibility, and \emph{unit margin}. The former is the standard notion
of inducibility, and the latter asks that every arc be carried by
exactly three voters against two. Only an unrestricted refutation bounds
\(N(5)\). However, the lemma below shows that for the Paley family and
most tournaments derived from it, the unrestricted inducibility is
equivalent to inducibility with margin at most \(3\) (i.e.~support at
most 4).

We also use unit margin as a fast filter for unrestricted inducibility.
A unit-margin witness is in particular a majority witness, and the
unit-margin search is much faster (Appendix F.4), so when the question
is only whether a tournament is \(5\)-inducible we search at unit margin
first and at plain majority only if no unit-margin witness exists. The
order-\(12\) analysis of Appendix A works this way, and so do several of
the family searches on \(21\) vertices (Appendix C). A tournament that
fails the filter but has a majority witness is then a unit-margin
obstruction found as a by-product, and several of those reported below
arose this way.

\textbf{Lemma 6.1 (3-cycle bound).} \emph{Let \(u \to v \to w \to u\) be
a directed triangle of \(T\) and let \(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_k\) be any linear
orders with \(k\) odd. Then}
\[c(u\to v) + c(v \to w) + c(w \to u) \le 2k.\] \emph{Consequently, in
any majority witness, no arc lying in a directed triangle has unanimous
support \(k\).}

\emph{Proof.} A linear order cannot rank \(u\) above \(v\), \(v\) above
\(w\) and \(w\) above \(u\) simultaneously, so each voter agrees with at
most two of the three arcs; summing over the \(k\) voters gives the
inequality. If \(c(u \to v) = k\), the other two supports sum to at most
\(k\), contradicting the majority requirement that each be at least
\((k+1)/2\). \(\blacksquare\)

For \(k = 3\) the second conclusion is Proposition 1 of Milosz, Hamel
and Pierrot (2018), with a transitivity argument; we need it for every
odd \(k\), and in the summed form above.

\subsubsection{6.1 Paley(23) and Paley(31) are not
5-inducible}\label{paley23-and-paley31-are-not-5-inducible}

\textbf{Theorem 6.2.} \emph{\(P_{23}\) is not \(5\)-inducible.
Consequently \(N(5) \le 23\).}

The proof is a search that runs to completion. The base is
\(\{1,2,3,6,12\}\), which induces the regular tournament on \(5\)
vertices and gives \(8{,}031\) base states. They are reduced to
\(2{,}591\) by Lemma 5.1(b), by requiring one voter to rank either
\((1,3)\) or \((1,6)\) as the first pair. None of them produces a
witness. This statement is also formally certified in Appendix B.1.

Thus none of the \(23!^{5}\) five-voter profiles on \(23\) vertices has
\(P_{23}\) as its majority tournament. It is the first explicit
tournament of order below \(43\) shown not to be \(5\)-inducible, and
the first whose non-inducibility a general-purpose solver could not
settle.

The same method settles a larger case: \(P_{31}\) is not
\(5\)-inducible, over \(21{,}009\) base states with a different base,
reduced to \(4{,}007\) by Lemma 5.1(b). Appendix F.4 shows how the
runtime scales in \(q\), and Appendix F.4 tabulates the cost of every
computation in this paper. Settling \(P_{31}\) shows that our search
decides \(31\)-vertex instances on a single laptop within a day, which
the SAT and ILP solvers we tried did not.

Arc-criticality implies vertex-criticality: reversing an arc incident to
\(v\) leaves \(T - v\) unchanged, and the restriction of a witness to
\(V \setminus \{v\}\) is a witness for \(T - v\). Each cell of Table 3
therefore records only the \emph{strongest} property established, at
each of the two margins. ``Not vertex-critical'' also rules out
arc-criticality.

\subsubsection{6.2 Paley(19) is not 5-inducible with unit
margin}\label{paley19-is-not-5-inducible-with-unit-margin}

That \(P_{19}\) is \(5\)-inducible is due to Bachmeier et al.~(2019).
Our search recovers it independently and contributes the negative half:
it is \emph{not} \(5\)-inducible with unit margin. This is a complete
refutation over all \(2{,}200\) base states of the base
\(\{1,2,3,4,6\}\), and it is the second statement we certify formally in
Appendix B.1. These two facts supply the separation in Section 1: no arc
of \(P_{19}\) can be unanimous, by the \(3\)-cycle bound above, so it is
\(5\)-inducible with margin at most \(3\) and not with unit margin.

\subsubsection{6.3 Paley(23) is arc-critical, unlike Paley(31) and
Paley(43)}\label{paley23-is-arc-critical-unlike-paley31-and-paley43}

\textbf{Theorem 6.3.} \emph{\(P_{23}\) is arc-critical, and hence
vertex-critical.}

As \(\mathrm{Aut}(P_{23})\) is transitive on arcs, it suffices to
reverse the arc \((1,2)\). The result, \(T^{e}\), is rigid, so the
search scans all \(8{,}031\) base states without anchoring, and the
witness it finds is checked independently: its five orders are
permutations of the \(23\) vertices whose majority tournament agrees
with \(T^{e}\) on all \(253\) arcs. The two notions come apart among the
four vertex-transitive unit-margin obstructions on \(21\) vertices: all
four are vertex-critical, but two have every arc critical, one exactly
half and one none (Appendix H).

\textbf{Theorem 6.4.} \emph{Neither \(P_{31}\) nor \(P_{43}\) is
vertex-critical for 5-inducibility.}

\emph{Proof.} \(\mathrm{Aut}(P_{q} - v)\) is the stabilizer of \(v\) in
\(\mathrm{Aut}(P_{q})\): an automorphism of \(P_q - v\) preserves its
two out-degrees, \((q-1)/2\) on \(N^{+}(v)\) and \((q-1)/2 - 1\) on
\(N^{-}(v)\), hence those two sets, and so extends to \(P_q\) by fixing
\(v\). With \(v = 0\) the stabilizer is
\(\{x \mapsto ax : a \in \mathrm{QR}\}\), whose vertex orbits are
\(\mathrm{QR}\) and its complement, so vertex anchoring (Lemma 5.1(c))
applies with one representative from each. Every arc of \(P_q - v\) lies
in at least \((q-3)/4\) directed triangles, so by Lemma 6.1 margin at
most \(3\) is no restriction, and a complete search at that margin over
all \(8{,}031\) base states of a five-vertex base inducing the regular
tournament on five vertices, \(\{1,2,3,4,13\}\) in \(P_{31} - v\) and
\(\{1,2,3,4,11\}\) in \(P_{43} - v\), finds no witness. \(\blacksquare\)

This reconfirms Theorem 4.1 independently, since a witness for
\(P_{43}\) would restrict to one for \(P_{43} - v\).

\subsubsection{6.5 Eight tournaments on 22 vertices are not
5-inducible}\label{eight-tournaments-on-22-vertices-are-not-5-inducible}

None of the \(37\) doubly regular tournaments on \(23\) vertices in the
catalogue of McKay (2026) is \(5\)-inducible (Table 4), which makes
their one-vertex deletions natural candidates. We write \(\Delta_i\) for
the \(i\)-th, with vertices \(v_1, \dots, v_{23}\) in the catalogue's
labelling, and \(\Delta_i - v_j\) for \(\Delta_i\) with \(v_j\) deleted.

\textbf{Theorem 6.5.} \emph{The tournaments \(\Delta_1 - v_{1}\),
\(\Delta_1 - v_{2}\), \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\), \(\Delta_6 - v_{5}\),
\(\Delta_{11} - v_{2}\), \(\Delta_{11} - v_{5}\),
\(\Delta_{11} - v_{20}\) and \(\Delta_{28} - v_{5}\), on \(22\)
vertices, are not \(5\)-inducible. In particular, no tournament obtained
from \(\Delta_1\) by deleting one vertex is \(5\)-inducible.
Consequently \(N(5) \le 22\).}

The second statement holds because \(\mathrm{Aut}(\Delta_1)\), of order
\(55\), has three vertex orbits, of sizes \(11\), \(11\) and \(1\),
containing \(v_1\), \(v_2\) and \(v_{10}\). The eight are pairwise
non-isomorphic, even up to converse, and each has eleven vertices of
out-degree \(10\) and eleven of out-degree \(11\); every arc lies in at
least five directed triangles, so by the \(3\)-cycle bound no arc of a
witness is unanimous. Appendix G gives their adjacency matrices.

Deleting a vertex \(v\) of a doubly regular tournament on \(23\)
vertices leaves two regular \emph{halves} on \(11\) vertices,
\(N^{+}(v)\) and \(N^{-}(v)\), each out-neighbour of \(v\) beating \(6\)
in-neighbours and each in-neighbour \(5\) out-neighbours. We call \(v\)
a \emph{bisector} if its halves are isomorphic, or converse, to each
other (an \emph{isomorphic} or a \emph{converse} bisector), and a
tournament \emph{bisectable} if it has a bisector or arises from one by
deleting a bisector; one tournament on half the vertices then fixes both
halves, which makes bisectable tournaments much easier to enumerate.
Every doubly regular tournament on at most \(23\) vertices is
bisectable, whereas \(118\) of the \(722\) on \(27\) vertices are not.
Our obstructions cover every case. In \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\) and
\(\Delta_{11} - v_{20}\) both halves are \(P_{11}\); in the four other
deletions of \(\Delta_1\) and \(\Delta_{11}\) they are isomorphic to
each other and to each other's converse, with automorphism groups of
order \(5\); in \(\Delta_{28} - v_{5}\) they are converses of each
other, with trivial automorphism groups; and in \(\Delta_6 - v_{5}\)
they are neither, and no other split works, so \(\Delta_6 - v_{5}\) is
non-bisectable.

Figure 1 shows \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\) as two copies of \(P_{11}\) on
\(X = \{x_0, \dots, x_{10}\}\) and \(Y = \{y_0, \dots, y_{10}\}\) joined
by a ladder. Its automorphism group, of order \(55\), has vertex orbits
\(X\) and \(Y\), and the automorphisms multiplying every index by a
non-zero square fix \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) and form a subgroup \(H\) of
order \(5\) mapping \(B = \{x_1, x_3, x_4, x_5, x_9\}\), the
out-neighbours of \(x_0\) in \(X\), onto itself. \(B\) induces the
regular tournament on five vertices, whose \(8{,}031\) base states \(H\)
groups into \(1{,}611\) orbits, so Lemma 5.1(e) keeps one per orbit
while anchoring on \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) (Lemma 5.1(c)); the complete
search over the \(1{,}611\) finds no witness. The certificate (Appendix
B.1) uses the quotient without anchoring on
\(B' = \{y_1, y_3, y_4, y_5, y_9\}\), the in-neighbours of \(y_0\) in
\(Y\), again keeping \(1{,}611\) of the \(8{,}031\). The other five
deletions with an automorphism group of order \(5\), two of \(\Delta_1\)
and the three of \(\Delta_{11}\), whose group fixes \(v_2\), \(v_5\) and
\(v_{20}\), are refuted in the same way over \(1{,}611\) chosen base
states each, without certificates. \(\Delta_6 - v_{5}\) and
\(\Delta_{28} - v_{5}\) have trivial automorphism groups, so their
searches cover all \(8{,}031\) base states; \(\Delta_6 - v_{5}\) is also
certified (Appendix B.1).

All eight obstructions are \emph{vertex-critical}, so none yields a
\(21\)-vertex obstruction by a further deletion, and the wider search
below \(22\) vertices (Appendix C) has found none.

\textbf{A ninth obstruction.} The \(231\) arcs of \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\)
fall into five orbits: inside \(X\), inside \(Y\), the rungs
\(y_i \to x_i\), the other arcs from \(Y\) to \(X\), and the arcs from
\(X\) to \(Y\). Reversing a rung yields a \(5\)-inducible tournament,
but reversing an arc \(y_j \to x_i\) with \(j - i \in \mathrm{QR}\) does
not: the result is rigid, and a complete search over all \(8{,}031\)
base states finds no witness. This ninth obstruction is not isomorphic
to any of the eight or to their converses, and it is vertex-critical:
deleting an endpoint of the reversed arc leaves a deletion of
\(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\), and the other \(20\) deletions have explicit
witnesses. Whether reversing an arc of the remaining three orbits
restores \(5\)-inducibility is open, as is the arc-criticality of the
other seven; Section 7 collects the open questions.

\subsection{7. Discussion and open
problems}\label{discussion-and-open-problems}

In this section we state the questions that our results leave open
(Section 7.1) and describe how every result can be reproduced (Section
7.2).

\subsubsection{7.1 Conjectures and open
problems}\label{conjectures-and-open-problems}

\textbf{Question 1.} \emph{Is \(N(5) = 22\), that is, is every
tournament on \(21\) vertices \(5\)-inducible?}

The first version of this paper (arXiv:2609.13924v1), whose upper bound
was \(N(5) \le 23\), conjectured that \(N(5) = 23\). The obstructions of
Section 6.5 refute that conjecture, so we pose the question one order
lower.

Both ends of \(13 \le N(5) \le 22\) are constructive, unlike the
counting bound of Section 3. The unit-margin obstructions at \(n = 19\)
and \(21\) cannot move the upper one, since every one of them is
majority-inducible. The evidence below \(22\) comes mostly from the
descendants of the doubly regular tournaments on \(23\) vertices
(Appendix C): every tournament on \(20\) vertices obtained from one of
them by deleting three vertices is \(5\)-inducible, and so is every one
on \(21\) vertices below any of the nine \(22\)-vertex obstructions.

Every class we searched below \(22\) vertices was chosen because
symmetry, a construction, or descent from a known obstruction made it
searchable, and Appendix C lists them all. These classes cluster around
the doubly regular tournaments, where every obstruction we know of lies.
What they show is that no obstruction on \(21\) vertices lies
\emph{among them}; they say little about the roughly
\(3 \times 10^{43}\) tournaments on \(21\) vertices.

\textbf{Conjecture 2.} \emph{\(P_{q}\) is not \(5\)-inducible for any
eligible prime \(q \ge 23\), i.e.~\(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\).}

It holds at \(q = 23\), \(31\) and \(43\), and only finitely many \(q\)
are in doubt: \(\alpha^{*}(P_q) = \mathrm{MAS}(P_q)/C\) tends to
\(1/2\), because Paley tournaments are quasi-random in the sense of
Chung and Graham (1991, Section 6), and one of their equivalent
quasi-random properties (\(P_9\)) is that every linear order of the
vertices agrees with \((1/2 + o(1))\binom{q}{2}\) arcs. Hence the
predictability eventually falls below \(3/5\), past which the necessary
condition \(\alpha^{*} \ge 3/5\) refutes \(5\)-inducibility with no
search at all. What is missing is a bound on where that range ends; the
values in Table 2 fall too irregularly to extrapolate one.

\textbf{Open problem 1 (vertex- and arc-criticality).} Whether \(P_{q}\)
minus \emph{two} vertices is \(5\)-inducible, equivalently whether
\(P_{q}\) minus one vertex is vertex-critical, is open at \(q = 31\) and
\(q = 43\). For \(q = 43\), \(P_{43} - 2v\) is unique up to isomorphism,
since \(\mathrm{Aut}(P_{43})\) acts transitively on unordered pairs of
vertices, and the screen of Section 4.2 extends to it. The two deleted
vertices can be chosen after the screen rather than before: a pair of
orders survives the deletion exactly when some set of at most two
vertices meets every cyclic triangle that both orders double-back, a
property invariant under \(\mathrm{Aut}\), so the reduction by a factor
of \(903\) survives the deletion. The condition on the two voters that
agree with the most arcs sharpens to
\(\delta_1 + 4 \delta_2 \le 5\,\mathrm{MAS} - 3C\), where
\(\delta_1, \delta_2\) are their levels; the right-hand side is \(45\)
for \(P_{43} - 2v\).

\textbf{Open problem 2 (the margin hierarchy).} The first of unit margin
\(\subseteq\) margin \(\le 3\) \(\subseteq\) unrestricted is strict
(Sections 6.2 and 6.4); whether the second ever is, that is, whether
some tournament is inducible by five voters but not at margin \(\le 3\),
is open. Lemma 6.1 forces such a tournament to have an arc in \emph{no}
directed triangle, which rules out every regular tournament. Conjecture
8.1 of our companion paper states that the inclusion is strict at every
odd \(k > 3\).

\textbf{Conjecture 3.} \emph{For every odd \(k\), every \(k\)-inducible
tournament is \((k+2)\)-inducible with unit margin.}

Conjecture 3 extends Conjecture 8.1 of our companion paper, which
concerns the classes for a single \(k\), to classes across different
\(k\). Adding an order together with its reverse raises every support by
one, so \(k\)-inducibility with margin at most \(M\) implies
\((k+2)\)-inducibility with margin at most \(M\). Conjecture 3
strengthens this to unit margin, and with the inclusions within each
\(k\) it would nest every class at a lower \(k\) inside every class at a
higher \(k\), whatever the margins. It trivially holds at \(k = 1\),
since a transitive tournament is the majority of an order taken twice
and its reverse once, with every arc carried two against one. At
\(k = 3\), Appendix A shows that every tournament on at most \(12\)
vertices is \(5\)-inducible with unit margin, so a counterexample needs
at least \(13\) vertices. At \(k = 5\), both doubly regular tournaments
on \(19\) vertices, which are \(5\)-inducible but not with unit margin,
are \(7\)-inducible with unit margin.

\subsubsection{7.2 Reproducibility}\label{reproducibility}

The code, data and verdict files are at
\url{https://github.com/Leonardini/TournamentsBeyond5Voters}. Its
\texttt{CLAIMS.md} carries one row per statement made here, naming the
artifact that holds the verdict and the command that re-derives it, and
it ends with an explicit list of what the package does not establish. A
permanent archive of the accepted version will be deposited on
acceptance, with its DOI recorded here.

The repository contains the search engine as a single C translation
unit, the SAT encoding and cube tooling in Python, the certification
driver, the independent witness verifiers, the tournaments as bit
strings, and a verdict record for every result reported here. Each
verdict records the exact command line, the base and break used, the
coverage audit (the check that the cubes miss no base state, Appendix
B.2), the runtime, and the two root hashes for the formally certified
results. Three further folders hold the counting bounds
(\texttt{counting\_bounds/}), the proof for \(P_{43}\), which rebuilds
from one script (\texttt{paley43/}), and every check that the
tournaments on at most \(12\) vertices are \(5\)-inducible with unit

Of the two root hashes published with each certified computation, ROOT

\subsection{Appendix A. The order-12 case
analysis}\label{appendix-a.-the-order-12-case-analysis}

Every tournament on \(12\) vertices is \(5\)-inducible, so
\(N(5) \ge 13\). This appendix describes that computation, which starts
from a census of order \(11\). It is one of the results in this paper
that was not run on the laptop of Section 6: it ran on a shared cluster,
and its runtime is converted as Appendix F.4 describes.

\subsubsection{A.1 Every tournament on eleven vertices is
5-inducible}\label{a.1-every-tournament-on-eleven-vertices-is-5-inducible}

Our first census shows that every tournament on at most \(11\) vertices
is \(5\)-inducible, so \(N(5) \ge 12\). Through \(n = 10\) each
tournament is decided by the integer program of Appendix F.1. At
\(n = 11\) no integer program was needed: of the
\(D_{11} = 903{,}753{,}248\) tournaments, \(382{,}625{,}248\) are
3-inducible by the triple-local test of our companion paper, and every
one of the remaining \(521{,}128{,}000\) admits a \emph{unit-margin}
five-voter profile, found by a constraint search and re-checked
independently. A tournament with a non-3-inducible subtournament is
itself non-3-inducible, and restricting a profile to a subset of the
vertices preserves every pairwise split, so every non-3-inducible
tournament on at most \(11\) vertices is \(5\)-inducible with unit
margin.

\subsubsection{A.2 Reduction to one-vertex
extensions}\label{a.2-reduction-to-one-vertex-extensions}

A direct census is out of reach: \(D_{12} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{11}\)
classes, \(170\) times the order-\(11\) census of Appendix A.1. We use
that census as the outer loop. We write \(L + S\) for the tournament
obtained from an order-\(11\) tournament \(L\) by adding a vertex \(v\)
whose out-neighbourhood is \(S \subseteq V(L)\). Two observations
simplify the analysis.

\textbf{(i)} Every order-\(12\) tournament is \(L + S\) for some
order-\(11\) \(L\) and some \(S\).

\textbf{(ii)} Restricting a profile that realizes \(L + S\) to \(V(L)\)
gives a profile that realizes \(L\).

By (i), enumerating all \(D_{11}\) order-\(11\) classes and settling all
\(2{,}048\) extensions of each settles every order-\(12\) tournament;
all of the former are \(5\)-inducible by the census of Appendix A.1,
which is what makes the outer loop available. By (ii), a profile
realizing \(L + S\) may be sought by \emph{inserting} \(v\) into a
profile realizing \(L\) with no loss of generality, since every profile
for \(L+S\) arises that way. That is what phase 1 uses.

\subsubsection{A.3 Three phases}\label{a.3-three-phases}

The census runs in three phases, fastest first.

\textbf{Phase 1, the covering search.} Rather than \(2{,}048\) separate
instances per order-\(11\) tournament, one search enumerates profiles
realizing \(L\) \emph{at unit margin} and, for each, records every
out-neighbourhood obtainable by inserting \(v\) into its five orders:
\(v\)'s position in each order is a threshold, and the support of
\(v \to u\) is the number of orders that place \(v\) above \(u\), so one
traversal certifies many extensions at once. This is the engine of
Section 5.2 with \(v\)'s placement read off rather than fixed in
advance. The margin makes this phase pay twice: it keeps the enumeration
of profiles for \(L\) small, and a unit-margin witness is in particular
a majority witness, so every extension the phase covers is settled in
both regimes at once. The phase is one-sided: an extension it covers is
certified \(5\)-inducible, but one left over is not refuted. This phase
settles every one of the \(2{,}048\) extensions of \(99.5\%\) of the
order-\(11\) tournaments.

\textbf{Phase 2, deciding what is left.} Each extension phase 1 leaves
open is materialized as an order-\(12\) tournament \(L + S\) and decided
by the same engine, again at unit margin, where a witness settles it
positively. About one extension in \(200{,}000\) reached this phase, and
every one of them is \(5\)-inducible with unit margin.

\textbf{Phase 3, plain majority.} Only a \emph{majority} refutation
would be a counterexample, so any instance negative at unit margin is
re-run without the margin restriction. Phase 3 was never invoked: every
tournament was settled at unit margin, so the weaker question never had
to be asked.

\subsubsection{A.4 One tournament of each converse
pair}\label{a.4-one-tournament-of-each-converse-pair}

\(T\) is \(5\)-inducible if and only if its converse \(\bar{T}\) is
(Section 1). Moreover
\(\overline{L + S} = \bar{L} + (V(L) \setminus S)\), so settling all
\(2{,}048\) extensions of \(L\) settles all \(2{,}048\) of \(\bar{L}\)
as well. We therefore keep one tournament of each converse pair, by the
rule that \(L\) is kept if and only if
\(\mathrm{canon}(L) \le \mathrm{canon}(\bar{L})\). This keeps exactly
one of each pair together with every self-converse tournament,
\((D_{11} + S_{11})/2\) in all, with \(S_{11} = 279{,}968\); this saves
a factor of \(\lessapprox 2\). We also check the number of tournaments
kept is \((D_{11} + S_{11})/2\).

Total runtime: \(2{,}615.6\) core-hours. The search code, the per-part
records and the roll-up are at \texttt{n12\_unit\_margin/n12\_cover} in
the repository of Section 7.2.

\subsubsection{B.1 The certified
refutations}\label{b.1-the-certified-refutations}

The four certified refutations follow an established design, and we
claim no methodological novelty. \emph{Cube-and-conquer} (Heule,
Kullmann, Wieringa and Biere 2012) splits a hard instance into partial
assignments, the \emph{cubes}, each handed to a CDCL solver; the
standard of proof is that of the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem
(Heule et al.~2016), later re-checked with a formally verified checker
(Cruz-Filipe et al.~2019), of Schur number five (Heule 2018) and of the
Keller conjecture (Brakensiek et al.~2020), in which every cube's
refutation is validated by an independent checker. Each cube is refuted
by CaDiCaL 2.0.0 (\texttt{-\/-lrat=true\ -\/-checkproof=0}), which
writes an LRAT proof (Cruz-Filipe et al. 2017): each derived clause
lists the earlier clauses it follows from, so \texttt{lrat-trim}
(Pollitt et al.~2023) checks it without search. Our cubes are base
states, a combinatorial decomposition, so the coverage of the cube set
is certified separately (Appendix B.2). Where Heule et al.~(2016)
published a cube certificate from which the refutations can be
regenerated, at about \(13{,}000\) core-hours to regenerate and
\(16{,}000\) to check, we \emph{verify and discard}: each LRAT proof is
checked, hashed and deleted, and only the hash is published (Section
7.2). Table B.1 summarizes the four certificates.

has found none.

subtournaments: restricting a witness for \(T\) to a subset of the
vertices gives a witness for the subtournament. An obstruction on fewer

tournaments of each order are first screened for witnesses, and only
tournaments that survive the screen are searched further.

\subsection{Appendix D. The MAS algorithm: orbit subset-DP,
meet-in-the-middle, and the negation/converse join
key}\label{appendix-d.-the-mas-algorithm-orbit-subset-dp-meet-in-the-middle-and-the-negationconverse-join-key}

This appendix describes the dynamic program that certifies
\(\mathrm{MAS}(P_{43}) = 543\) (Section 4.1) and enumerates the
level-\(\le 1\) shell (Section 4.2).

\textbf{D.1 Base recurrence (Held--Karp 1962).} For \(S \subseteq V\)
let \(g(S) = \mathrm{MAS}(T[S])\). The last vertex \(v\) of an order of
\(S\) gains its in-arcs from \(S \setminus \{v\}\), so
\[g(\varnothing) = 0, \qquad g(S) = \max_{v \in S}\bigl[\, g(S \setminus \{v\}) + |\{u \in S \setminus \{v\} : u \to v\}| \,\bigr],\]
and \(\mathrm{MAS}(T) = g(V)\).

\textbf{D.2 Automorphism quotient.} As \(T[\sigma S] \cong T[S]\) for
\(\sigma \in \mathrm{Aut} =
\{x \mapsto ax+b : a \in \mathrm{QR}\}\), we keep one representative per
orbit: \(4.9 \times 10^{9}\) states up to \(|S| = 21\) (almost \(903\)
times fewer). The representative is the minimum bit-mask over the images
that send a vertex of maximum out-degree within \(S\) to \(0\). Layer
\(j+1\) comes from layer \(j\): a stored \(S\) yields, for each
\(v \notin S\), the record
\((\mathrm{canon}(S \cup \{v\}),\, g(S) + |\{u \in S : u \to v\}|)\),
with the gain computed before canonicalizing; equal keys keep the
largest value, \(g(S \cup \{v\})\).

\textbf{D.3 Meet-in-the-middle at the equator.} Storing every layer up
to \(|S| = 43\) is out of reach, so we build layers \(0\) to \(22\) only
and split every order in the middle. Let \(\pi\) be an order of \(V\)
and \(S\) the set of its first \(21\) vertices (the \textbf{equator} is
\(h = 21\)). An arc of \(T\) lies inside \(S\), inside
\(V \setminus S\), or between the two, and an arc between them is
forward in \(\pi\) exactly when it goes from \(S\) to \(V \setminus S\).
Hence
\[\mathrm{fwd}(\pi) = \mathrm{fwd}(\pi|_S) + \mathrm{cross}(S) + \mathrm{fwd}(\pi|_{V \setminus S}),\]
where \(\mathrm{cross}(S)\) counts the arcs from \(S\) to
\(V \setminus S\), and maximizing the two halves separately gives
\[\mathrm{MAS}(T) = \max_{|S| = 21} \bigl[\, g(S) + \mathrm{cross}(S) + g(V \setminus S) \,\bigr].\]
The first term is read from layer \(21\). The suffix \(V \setminus S\)
has \(22\) vertices, but the tables were built from the front of an
order, so we read the suffix through a twin order. Negation
\(x \mapsto -x\) maps \(T\) onto its converse, because \(-1\) is a
non-residue; so reversing \(\pi\) and negating every vertex gives an
order \(\pi^{*}\) with \(\mathrm{fwd}(\pi^{*}) = \mathrm{fwd}(\pi)\),
whose first \(22\) vertices form the set \(-(V \setminus S)\). In
particular \(g(V \setminus S) = g(-(V \setminus S))\), which layer
\(22\) stores under the key \(\mathrm{canon}(-(V \setminus S))\).

\textbf{D.4 The join: the negation/complement commutes with the quotient
by \(\mathrm{Aut}\).} Layer \(21\) holds one representative per orbit,
so the maximum above must be taken over orbits, and the key of the
suffix must not depend on which representative was stored. Let
\(\nu(S) = -S\), \(\kappa(S) = V \setminus S\) and
\(\varphi = \nu \circ \kappa\), so that the suffix of \(S\) is looked up
at \(\mathrm{canon}(\varphi(S))\). (i) \(\kappa\) commutes with every
bijection of \(V\); (ii)
\(\nu \circ \sigma_{a,b} = \sigma_{a,-b} \circ \nu\), so negation
normalizes \(\mathrm{Aut}\) while lying outside it; hence (iii)
\(\varphi \circ \sigma_{a,b} = \sigma_{a,-b} \circ \varphi\). So if
\(S\) and \(S'\) lie in one orbit, so do \(\varphi(S)\) and
\(\varphi(S')\): \(\mathrm{canon}(\varphi(S))\) is constant on orbits,
and \(\varphi\) induces a one-to-one map from the orbits of \(21\)-sets
to the orbits of \(22\)-sets. As \(g\) and \(\mathrm{cross}\) are
constant on orbits as well, the maximum over all \(21\)-sets equals the
maximum over the stored representatives.

We stream layer \(21\) and form, for each representative \(S\), the pair
\((\mathrm{canon}(\varphi(S)),\, g(S) + \mathrm{cross}(S))\); we sort
these pairs by key and merge them with the stored layer-\(22\) table,
which is sorted by the same key. Each match gives
\(\mu(S) = g(S) + \mathrm{cross}(S) + g(\varphi(S))\), the largest
number of forward arcs of an order whose first \(21\) vertices form
\(S\), and \(\mathrm{MAS}(T) = \max_S \mu(S) = 543\). Backtracking
through the stored values from the splits with \(\mu(S) \ge 542\) lists
every order of level \(\le 1\), the shell of Section 4.2 (orbit counts
in Appendix E).

\subsection{Appendix F. The search
engine}\label{appendix-f.-the-search-engine}

Integer programming and satisfiability are both central to this project.
Every optimum reported in the companion paper is certified by an integer
or linear program, and the integer program of Appendix F.1 below is that
paper's \(k\)-inducibility formulation with antisymmetry substituted
out; the only machine-checked theorem in this paper is a SAT computation
from end to end, with CaDiCaL (Biere et al.~2024; Pollitt et al.~2023)
emitting LRAT proofs that \texttt{lrat-trim} (Pollitt et al.~2023)
checks. The claim here is narrow: on this one decision problem, at these
sizes, neither tool reaches the answer while a search built around the
problem's own structure does, and the same SAT machinery is then
indispensable for turning that answer into a proof.

\subsubsection{F.1 Integer programming and SAT are both too
slow}\label{f.1-integer-programming-and-sat-are-both-too-slow}

The natural integer program has one binary \(x_{v,ij}\) per voter \(v\)
and pair \(i < j\), meaning that \(v\) ranks \(i\) above \(j\), with
\(x_{v,ji}\) read as \(1 - x_{v,ij}\); transitivity rows
\(0 \le x_{v,ij} + x_{v,jl} - x_{v,il} \le 1\) for \(i < j < l\); and
one majority row \(\sum_{v} x_{v,ij} \ge (k+1)/2\) per arc. At
\(q = 23\), \(k = 5\) it has \(1{,}265\) binaries, \(17{,}710\)
transitivity rows and \(253\) majority rows. Setting \(x_{v,ij} = 3/5\)
on every arc of \(T\) and \(2/5\) on every non-arc satisfies every row,
so the linear programming (LP) relaxation is feasible and, the problem
having no objective, gives branch-and-bound nothing to prune by; that
point is invariant under both symmetry groups, so it gives no branching
direction either. CPLEX and Gurobi both failed to close instances at
\(n = 19\) within a day.

The satisfiability encoding is \emph{dissent-Boolean}: one variable per
voter \(v\) and arc of \(T\), true when \(v\) dissents from the arc.
Each arc has at most \((k-1)/2\) dissents for majority and exactly
\((k-1)/2\) for unit margin, and for every voter and triple the two
dissent patterns that would make the triple cyclic are forbidden. For
\(k = 5\) that is \(5\binom{n}{2}\) variables, \(15\) clauses per arc at
unit margin and \(10\) per triple, and the certified refutations of
Appendix B are built on it. On uniform random tournaments the
\texttt{Sat-Check-k-Majority} of Bachmeier et al.~(2019, Table 4)
averages \(0.23\) seconds at \(n = 18\) and \(18.95\) at \(n = 23\),
roughly doubling per vertex, whereas on \(P_{23}\) the same solver did
not terminate within six weeks: random tournaments have no automorphisms
to rediscover and many witnesses. Better encodings and explicit symmetry
breaks help but do not suffice; supplying the decomposition into base
states as cubes does (Section 5.1).

\subsubsection{F.2 The symmetry reductions in
full}\label{f.2-the-symmetry-reductions-in-full}

In this subsection we prove Lemma 5.1 and explain how its parts are
used.

\emph{Proof of Lemma 5.1.} (a) Let \(w\) be a witness and choose
\(g \in G\) with \(g\,\varphi(w) \in R\). Then \(gw\) is a witness,
since permuting voters changes no margin and relabelling by an
automorphism carries the margin of each arc to its image, and
\(\varphi(gw) = g\,\varphi(w) \in R\). Parts (b) and (c) are (a) with
\(G = \mathrm{Aut}(T)\), \(\varphi(\pi)\) the top pair, respectively the
top vertex, of \(\pi_1\), and \(R\) the representatives. Part (d) is (a)
with \(G = S_k\), \(\varphi\) the identity and \(R\) the sorted
profiles, every \(S_k\)-orbit containing one. A second application of
(a) may use any group element that preserves what the first guaranteed,
and permuting voters preserves ``some voter ranks \(a_i\) first and
\(b_i\) second'' (or ``\(v_i\) first''), which depends on the multiset
of orders and not on which voter carries it; so (d) applies after (b) or
(c). For (e), relabelling by \(h \in H\) maps a base state of \(C\) to
one, since \(h(C) = C\) and \(h\) restricts to an automorphism of
\(T[C]\), while \(S_k\) acts trivially on base states, which are
multisets; and the base state of \(C\) that \(h\pi\) extends is \(h\)
applied to the one \(\pi\) extends. So (a) with \(G = S_k \times H\) and
\(\varphi(\pi)\) that base state gives a witness extending a chosen base
state of \(C\), and its orders restricted to \(B\) form a chosen base
state of \(B\) that it extends. If \(H\) fixes the representatives, each
\(h \in H\) maps an anchored voter to an anchored voter, so the same
argument applies after (b) or (c). \(\blacksquare\)

Part (b) guarantees only that \emph{some} \(i\) works, not any
particular one, and it is this weak form that lets (d) follow it; had it
said ``voter \(1\) ranks \(a_i\) first'', the composition would fail.
For a Paley tournament there are two orbits on ordered pairs, the arcs
and the non-arcs: the maps \(x \mapsto a(x-t)\) with
\(a \in \mathrm{QR}\) are automorphisms, they send \((t,s)\) to
\((0, a(s-t))\), and as \(a\) ranges over \(\mathrm{QR}\) they leave
exactly two orbits, separated by whether \(s - t \in \mathrm{QR}\). On
\(P_7\) (Figure F.2) the representatives are \((1,2)\) and \((1,4)\).
Otherwise we compute \(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\) and its orbits with
\texttt{nauty} 2.8.6 (McKay and Piperno 2014), which also provides every
automorphism group, orbit set and canonical form reported in this paper;
(b) and (c) are alternatives, and the better one depends on the numbers
of orbits on pairs and on vertices.

When every representative pair lies inside \(B\), a voter ranking
\(a_i\) first and \(b_i\) second overall does so within \(B\), which the
base state reveals, so a base state in which no voter tops \(B\) with a
chosen pair extends to no anchored witness and may be discarded; the
survivors are the \emph{live} base states. Otherwise the search tests
the anchor once every representative has both endpoints placed: an
insertion can destroy an anchor but never create one, so a partial
profile with no anchored voter has no anchored extension. The SAT
certification tests it only once, to decide which cubes reach the
solver.

In part (e) the condition on the representatives cannot be dropped: if
\(H\) moves one, mapping an anchored witness into a chosen base state
can destroy its anchor. Automorphism groups of tournaments have odd
order, since an automorphism of order \(2\) would swap the endpoints of
some arc, and an automorphism \(h\) maps \(B\) onto itself exactly when
\(B\) is a union of its cycles; a \(5\)-cycle of an automorphism of
order \(5\) is such a base. When no subgroup maps a base of the size we
use onto itself, \(C \subsetneq B\) helps: a fixed-point-free
automorphism of order \(3\) maps no five vertices onto itself but maps
each of its \(3\)-cycles onto itself, and with \(C\) one of them the
search keeps only the base states of \(B\) above a chosen base state of
\(C\), choosing in each orbit the one with fewest base states above it.
Three unit-margin searches of Appendix C kept \(600\) of \(2{,}200\) in
this way.

\textbf{Using the whole group.} We take \(H = G_R\), the common
stabilizer of the representatives. For a single representative \(r\) the
orbit--stabilizer theorem (Dummit and Foote 2004, Section 4.1,
Proposition 2) gives
\(|\mathrm{Aut}(T)| = |\mathrm{Aut}(T) \cdot r| \cdot |G_r|\), so
anchoring and the quotient between them use the whole group; with
several representatives we choose them to keep \(G_R\) large, and the
base among the unions of cycles of \(G_R\) or the sets containing one.
Table F.1 shows the split: for the Paley tournaments the stabilizer of a
representative pair is trivial, so the whole group goes to anchoring,
and for \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\) it splits into orbits of \(11\) and a
stabilizer of order \(5\); Figure F.1 summarizes which parts of Lemma
5.1 apply to a given tournament and base.

\subsubsection{F.3 The five steps on a small
example}\label{f.3-the-five-steps-on-a-small-example}

Given \(T\) and the margin regime, the search has five steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Pick a size.} Choose the base size \(b\), in practice \(5\) or
  \(6\).
\item
  \textbf{Pick a subtournament.} Choose a set \(B\) of \(b\) vertices.
  Only \(T[B]\) matters for what follows, so the choice can and should
  be optimized quickly (Section 5.3), since the base-state count varies
  by more than two orders of magnitude across the \(12\) isomorphism
  classes at \(b = 5\).
\item
  \textbf{Find its base states.} Enumerate the \(k\)-tuples of linear
  orders of \(B\) whose supports agree with \(T[B]\), counted up to
  voter permutation by Lemma 5.1(d).
\item
  \textbf{Determine the usable symmetries.} Compute \(\mathrm{Aut}(T)\)
  with \texttt{nauty} and its orbits, then impose the ordered-pair
  anchoring of Lemma 5.1(b), the vertex anchoring of Lemma 5.1(c), or
  neither, discarding the base states no anchored witness can use. If a
  subgroup \(H\) maps \(B\), or a subset \(C\) of it, onto itself, also
  keep only the base states above one representative of each \(H\)-orbit
  (Lemma 5.1(e)), checking that \(H\) fixes the anchoring
  representatives (Figure F.1).
\item
  \textbf{Run to completion, in parallel across base states.} The
  verdict is positive as soon as any base state yields a witness. It is
  negative only when \emph{every} live base state has been searched to
  exhaustion.
\end{enumerate}

fast; step 5 runs in parallel.

\subsubsection{F.4 Runtimes for every
computation}\label{f.4-runtimes-for-every-computation}

All runs used a single laptop (Apple M-series, 14 cores, 24 GB RAM, at
most 11 cores at once) except the following, which used a compute
cluster: the census of regular tournaments on \(15\) vertices, the
self-converse census of order \(13\), the order-\(12\) census of
Appendix A, six of the \(22\)-vertex sweeps of Section 6.5, part of the
certificate for \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\), \(16\) of the \(19\) unrestricted
sweeps of doubly regular tournaments on \(23\) vertices in Section 6.4,
and most of the search of Appendix C.

Runtimes are in \emph{core-hours}, the workers' running times summed.
The cluster ran \(2\) to \(5\) times slower per unit of work, so we
convert its runtimes to the laptop's scale as follows, unless marked as
cluster core-hours.

The certificate for \(\Delta_1 - v_{10}\) ran partly on each machine; on
the \(133\) groups of five cubes solved on both, the cluster took on
average \(2.1\) times as long (\(1.6\) to \(3.1\) for individual
groups), and we divide its cluster time by that average, so the
converted total is approximate. The searches of \(\Delta_1 - v_{1}\),
\(\Delta_1 - v_{2}\), \(\Delta_{11} - v_{5}\) and
\(\Delta_{11} - v_{20}\) run a different program: on \(355\) base states
of \(\Delta_{11} - v_{5}\) searched on both machines, the cluster took
\(3.55\) times as long in total, and we use that factor; we apply it
also to the sweeps of \(\Delta_6 - v_{5}\), \(\Delta_{28} - v_{5}\) and
\(16\) of the doubly regular tournaments on \(23\) vertices, for which
no paired measurement exists. The self-converse census of order \(13\)
ran partly on each machine, and there the cluster took \(5.13\) times as
long per tournament. The censuses of orders \(12\) and \(15\) ran on the
cluster alone; on random samples of \(8\) and \(12\) of their parts
rerun on the laptop, the cluster took \(3.85\) and \(4.28\) times as
long respectively. Table F.2 lists the result.
